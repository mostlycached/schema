# Site Catalog

Demonstrations of excavation. Each entry applies the system to a site and shows what shifts.

---

## 1. The Thing You Said That You Can't Unsay

### The Site

You're in conversation. Words leave your mouth. The moment they land, you know: *that shouldn't have been said*. But it's out. The other person heard it. You can't retrieve it.

### The Tension

The tension isn't regret — that comes after. The tension is *the gap between speaking and having-spoken*. For an instant you're in both states: still saying it, already having said it. Then the gap closes and you're stuck on the other side.

### Transfer: Cooking → Speech

Apply the structure of cooking-gone-wrong.

In cooking: you add salt. Too much. You can't remove it. You can only *compensate* — add more of other things, change the dish into something that needs the salt, or start over (if you can).

In speech: the unsayable-once-said works the same way. You can't remove it. You can only:
- **Compensate**: more words, reframing, "what I meant was..."
- **Transform the dish**: make the conversation *about* what you said, change the genre from "pleasant chat" to "difficult truth-telling"
- **Start over**: leave, wait, begin a new conversation later

**What shifts**: We usually think of apology as *undoing*. But cooking-structure reveals: apology is *compensation*, not removal. The salt is still in the dish. This is why some apologies fail — they promise removal when only compensation is possible.

### Generated Sites

Under the constraint "speech-acts that can only be compensated, not undone":
- **The secret you let slip** — compensation = "I shouldn't have told you that" (but now you know)
- **The insult delivered as joke** — compensation = "I was kidding" (but the content is out)
- **The love confession to the wrong person** — compensation impossible, only transformation ("let's pretend this didn't happen" is a genre-change)
- **The promise you can't keep** — future compensation required, present salt already added

## 2. The Room After Someone Has Left It

### The Site

Someone was here. Now they're not. The room is the same — same furniture, same light — but it's *saturated with absence*. Not empty. Full of not-them.

### The Tension

The room is materially unchanged but experientially transformed. You perceive presence *in the form of absence*. The chair they sat in. The indentation. The warmth fading. The tension: the room *should* feel the same but doesn't.

### Transfer: Inheritance → Presence

Apply the structure of inheritance (what's passed down without the person).

In inheritance: objects carry forward, but the person who gave meaning to them is gone. The heirloom works *because* the giver is absent — it becomes a vessel.

In the room: every object becomes an heirloom *before the person is dead*. The room inherits their presence *while they're still alive elsewhere*. You're bereaved in miniature, repeatedly.

**What shifts**: We usually think leaving is *subtraction*. But inheritance-structure reveals: leaving is *bequest*. The person deposits presence as they go. The room becomes richer, not emptier. This is why you can feel closer to someone in the room they just left than when they were in it.

### Generated Sites

Under the constraint "presence deposited as absence":
- **The bed still warm** — maximum deposit, minimum decay
- **The voicemail from someone dead** — presence perfectly preserved, person perfectly absent
- **The childhood home you return to** — decades of deposit layered
- **The half-finished cup they left** — deposit still liquid, still warm, time running out

## 3. The Apology That Made Things Worse

### The Site

You apologize. Sincerely. But as you speak, you watch the other person close off. Your apology *did something*, but not what you intended. Things are now worse than before you apologized.

### The Tension

The speech act failed — but not by misfiring. It fired correctly (you apologized, they recognized the apology) but achieved the opposite of its aim. The tension: you did the right thing wrong, or the wrong thing right.

### Transfer: Prescription → Apology

Apply the structure of medicine.

In medicine: the prescription is correct, but the patient has an reaction. The medicine isn't wrong — the interaction is. You treated the symptom but triggered something else.

In apology: the apology is correct (sincere, specific, accountable) but the person has a reaction. Your apology revealed you understood *what you did*, which means you *could have not done it*. The apology proves the wound was deliberate. Now they're angrier.

**What shifts**: We think apologies fail because they're insincere or incomplete. But prescription-structure reveals: apologies can fail because they're *too* accurate. Perfect understanding is evidence of available choice. This is why some people prefer "I don't know what I did wrong" — it offers the mercy of ignorance.

### Generated Sites

Under the constraint "accuracy worsens the wound":
- **The explanation that proves you knew better** — you weren't ignorant, you were choosing
- **The gift that shows you understand their taste** — proves you *could* have been thoughtful before, chose not to be
- **The late arrival with perfect excuse** — you could have planned this, why didn't you?
- **The remembered anniversary after years of forgetting** — the forgetting was choice, not incapacity

## 4. The Object You Can't Throw Away

### The Site

It's useless. It takes up space. You don't like it. Every time you clean, you consider throwing it out. You don't. It stays.

### The Tension

The tension isn't sentimentality — you don't *like* the object. It's that throwing it out would *mean something* you're not ready to mean. Discarding it would be a statement. Keeping it is also a statement. You're stuck in the grammar.

### Transfer: Clothing → Objects

Apply the structure of what you wear.

In clothing: you keep clothes that don't fit because *they fit once* or *might fit again*. The kept-clothing is a claim about the self: I am still the person who wore this, or could be.

In objects: the unkept object works the same way. The ticket stub, the gift from the ex, the broken thing. Keeping it says: I am still the person who went there, loved them, used this. Throwing it out says: I am no longer that person.

**What shifts**: We think keeping is about the object's value. But clothing-structure reveals: keeping is about *self-continuity*. You're not hoarding things; you're refusing to disown past selves. This is why decluttering is hard — it's ego-death in miniature, over and over.

### Generated Sites

Under the constraint "the object is a claim about the self":
- **The degree you never use** — throwing it out = I am not educated
- **The instrument you don't play** — throwing it out = I am not musical
- **The gift from the right person at the bad time** — throwing it out = they didn't matter
- **The project you'll never finish** — throwing it out = I will never be who I planned to be

## 5. The Name You've Forgotten But Should Know

### The Site

They approach. They clearly know you. They use your name. You *know* you know them. You cannot retrieve their name. You have to navigate the conversation without revealing this.

### The Tension

The tension is double: the cognitive failure (you *should* know) plus the performance (you must pretend you *do* know while trying to retrieve it). You're split between search and pretense.

### Transfer: Performance → Memory

Apply the structure of acting.

In acting: you play a character who knows things you don't. The actor doesn't know the character's childhood — but must behave as if they do. You impersonate knowledge.

In the name-gap: you're acting as someone who knows this person's name. But unlike theater, you're performing *as yourself*. You're impersonating your own competence. The memory failure forces you into self-performance.

**What shifts**: We usually think of forgetting as private failure. But performance-structure reveals: forgetting is *public exposure risk*. The terror isn't "I forgot" — it's "I might be caught having forgotten." This is why the name comes to you *after* they leave: the performance pressure was blocking retrieval.

### Generated Sites

Under the constraint "memory failure as performance crisis":
- **The story you've told before** — performing ignorance of your own repetition
- **The face you recognize but can't place** — performing composure while searching
- **The reference you should get** — performing understanding while not understanding
- **The language you used to speak** — performing your former self
